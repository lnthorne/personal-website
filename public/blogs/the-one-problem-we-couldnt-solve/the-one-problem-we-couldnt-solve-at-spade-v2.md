# The One Problem We Couldn't Solve at Spade

When we started Spade, I knew marketplaces were hard.

At least, I thought I knew.

We had heard all the warnings. Marketplaces are difficult to start. They are capital intensive. You have to solve supply and demand at the same time. You can build a great product and still end up with an empty marketplace. And yet, there we were: three guys convinced we were going to figure it out anyway.

Honestly, I am glad we were that naive. If I had fully understood how difficult the problem was before we started, maybe I would have never tried. And despite how things turned out, I don't regret trying for a second.

Spade taught me more about building products, marketing, distribution, marketplaces, and startups than I could have learned from any book or YouTube video. But there was one problem we could never solve.

For a while, I thought it was an organic marketing problem.

It wasn't.

The real problem was much harder: we needed to generate local homeowner demand at the exact moment someone needed a service, in a category where we had available providers, and we needed to do it economically and repeatedly.

We never figured out how.

## What we thought the cold-start problem was

Spade was a marketplace for homeowners to find local service providers. The basic experience was simple: a homeowner could post a job, local service providers could bid on it, and the homeowner could compare those providers, look through their work, read reviews, message them, and choose who they wanted to hire.

Going into it, I knew the consumer side would be difficult. I just didn't expect it to be _this_ difficult.

If I am being truthful, I thought the hard part was going to be building the product and getting enough good service providers onto the platform. Once that was done, I figured we could turn on some professionally made advertising, explain the value of the product, and homeowners would come.

Why wouldn't they?

We looked at the existing options and thought the experience was terrible. Many of the platforms were clunky, slow, confusing, and filled with homeowner complaints about the quality of the providers and the experience itself.

I was the technical founder, so naturally I looked at that and thought: _I can build something way better than this._

And I still think we did.

The problem was that we made a giant leap from:

> We can build a much better product.

to:

> Therefore, people will use it.

Those are not the same thing.

Our early "market research" did not exactly help. We mostly asked our parents, friends, and people close to us what they thought about the idea.

They liked it. Of course they did. They are our parents and friends.

At the time, we took that as validation. In hindsight, it was basically a textbook failure of [_The Mom Test_](https://medium.com/binsights/the-mom-test-by-rob-fitzpatrick-book-summary-d3e4ffd8b128). We were asking people whether they liked our solution instead of understanding how they were already solving the problem.

We should have been asking questions like:

- When was the last time you needed a plumber?
- How did you find them?
- How long did the process take?
- What was actually frustrating about it?
- How often do you hire someone to work on your home?
- What would make you change the way you already do it?

Instead, we mostly asked some version of:

> "Would you use this?"

People said yes. We believed them.

That would become a theme.

## Launch day came. Nobody came with it.

None of us had marketing experience. Not "a little marketing experience." Basically none.

We spent almost all of our time before launch building the product. Marketing was something we figured we would deal with once there was something worth marketing. That was our first major mistake.

When launch finally came, we made some pretty rough videos, posted them on Facebook and Instagram, and waited for the users to show up.

They did not.

We didn't really touch TikTok. We didn't use LinkedIn. We had decided that our target homeowners probably weren't there. What data did we have to support that?

Our parents didn't use those platforms.

So naturally, we extrapolated that to an entire city. Excellent market research.

After our organic content went almost nowhere, we moved to what seemed like the obvious next answer: Google Ads.

## Surely Google can find someone who needs a plumber

This one made perfect sense to us. If someone searched **"plumber near me"**, that person had intent. They were not scrolling Instagram half-interested in renovating their bathroom someday. They needed a plumber now.

That sounded perfect for Spade.

So we ran Google Ads.

Over 23 days, the campaign generated:

- **50,245 impressions**
- **29 attributed conversions**
- **$673.06 in spend**

And even the word _conversion_ was generous. Those conversions were downloads. Not accounts. Not homeowners posting jobs. Downloads.

We were celebrating activity much higher in the funnel than the thing that actually mattered.

Looking back, that was something we did over and over again. Impressions went up? Great. Website traffic went up? Great. Downloads increased? Great. Registrations increased? Great.

But there was really only one metric that mattered:

**Jobs posted.**

Everything else was a leading indicator at best and noise at worst. At the time, though, we didn't see it that way.

Google hadn't cracked the problem, so we decided to get a little more traditional.

## Maybe 3,000 pieces of cardboard will save us

We ordered 3,000 door flyers.

Actually, we ordered 6,000.

The first 3,000 had problems with the wording, branding, and QR code. And no, we did not use a dynamic QR code. That would have been far TOO sensible.

So now, armed with thousands of newly corrected flyers and a few younger cousins we had recruited with the promise of $30 and lunch, we hit the streets.

Our imagined ICP was roughly a homeowner over 30, under 75, affluent enough to spend money maintaining their home, busy enough that they didn't want to do the work themselves, and comfortable enough with technology to download an app.

We picked neighbourhoods where we thought those people lived and started walking. We handed out around 2,500 flyers.

We really thought these might work.

I don't know why.

We had just run a digital campaign that put Spade in front of more than 50,000 people and barely moved the needle. Then the same three people somehow convinced themselves that a few thousand pieces of cardstock were going to change the trajectory of the company.

They did not.

## Fine. Let's physically stand where the homeowners are.

Our next idea was home and garden shows. If we couldn't get homeowners to come to us, we would go directly to where thousands of homeowners were already gathered.

This time we went all in. We ordered banners. We had a big backdrop. Lights. A TV. A slideshow. The whole thing.

We really thought we were Apple unveiling the Apple II in 1977.

And to be fair, these shows were useful. They were fantastic for meeting service providers. We could walk from booth to booth, introduce ourselves, explain Spade, and bring providers onto the platform.

But provider liquidity was never really our biggest problem.

Homeowners were.

At the shows, we would talk to people about how they currently found contractors. We would show them Spade. They would tell us they loved the idea and that they would download it.

Then we would look at the analytics.

Turns out, saying you would download something and actually downloading it are two very different things.

I don't mean that those people were intentionally lying to us. They probably did like the idea. They probably did think they would use it someday. But "I like this" and "I am going to change my behaviour right now" are very different signals.

We didn't understand how different they were yet.

## So we hired people who actually knew what they were doing

Eventually, we looked at each other and admitted something important: we were not marketers.

Maybe the problem wasn't the opportunity. Maybe we were simply bad at communicating it.

So we brought in people who did this professionally. We hired a website and branding designer to replace our pretty rough Wix site, a digital marketing specialist, and a content creator.

We felt armed to the teeth.

And for a while, it looked like it was working. Our branding got better. Our content got better. We focused heavily on hyperlocal Meta advertising. We improved SEO. We ran Google brand campaigns. Website traffic increased. Downloads increased. Signups increased.

Our social content was generating around **300,000 impressions per month**, which felt enormous for a business operating in a single local market.

There was just one problem.

The jobs didn't increase at the same rate.

We were spending around **$5,000 per month** between marketing overhead and advertising. At the time, we justified that as the cost of breaking through the marketplace cold-start problem.

The thinking was basically: spend aggressively now, create enough homeowner demand, generate deal flow for providers, get the marketplace moving, then start monetizing the provider side.

That sounded reasonable to us.

But the funnel wasn't behaving the way we assumed it would. Homeowners would see an ad, visit the site, download the app, maybe create an account, and then...

Nothing.

Maybe one job would be posted for every 50 people who signed up.

That was the moment the real problem started becoming obvious.

## Your furnace doesn't break every Tuesday

This sounds unbelievably obvious now.

A homeowner does not need a plumber every Tuesday. Their furnace does not break every week. They don't need an electrician every second day. They might need a service provider a few times a year. For certain categories, maybe once every few years.

That changes everything.

We had been thinking about a homeowner registration as if we had acquired demand.

We hadn't.

We had acquired a person who _might have demand later_. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe six months from now. Maybe two years from now.

So even if we successfully paid to acquire that homeowner today, we now had another problem:

How do we remain top of mind until the exact moment something in their house breaks?

And because Spade was a local marketplace, generic demand wasn't enough either. We needed:

**the right homeowner + with a current need + in the right geography + for a category where we had supply + at roughly the same moment.**

That was the real cold-start problem.

Not "get a bunch of homeowners and providers to download an app."

We needed to continuously manufacture moments of local intent.

## The frustrating part: when Spade worked, it really worked

This is probably the part that was hardest for me.

When the marketplace had demand, it was awesome. A homeowner could post a job and, within ten minutes, have four bids from qualified service providers.

They could have conversations with them, look through reviews, portfolios, photos, social profiles, and previous work, compare their options, and choose the person they trusted.

It was genuinely fun to watch the lifecycle of a job happen on the platform.

We had spent so much time worrying that provider supply would kill us. It didn't. We had providers. We had built a product I was extremely proud of. People who actually used Spade often liked it, and many came back and posted again.

The problem was feeding the machine often enough.

A marketplace doesn't have demand because it has users.

It has demand when those users need something **right now**.

That distinction took me far too long to understand.

## So naturally, I built more features

When signups weren't turning into jobs quickly enough, we came up with another theory: maybe homeowners weren't getting to the "aha" moment fast enough. Maybe if the product were better, more people would post.

This is where being the technical founder probably worked against me.

Building was the thing I knew how to do, so I built more stuff.

Because that always works.

We added new AI features. I improved direct messaging. We enriched service-provider profiles. We polished the UI and UX. We made the product better in a lot of ways.

And almost none of it addressed the fire happening behind the scenes.

Looking back, I think there was a psychological reason for this. Engineering gave me a concrete problem. I could create a ticket, write the code, deploy it, and say, "Done."

Distribution was messy.

There was no compiler telling us what was wrong. There wasn't always a clear answer. Sometimes an idea failed because the channel was wrong. Sometimes the message was wrong. Sometimes the timing was wrong. Sometimes the entire underlying assumption was wrong.

So when we were faced with a difficult distribution problem, I retreated into the thing that made me feel productive.

I built.

That is probably one of the biggest lessons I will carry into whatever I build next.

## We eventually tried more channels. We were just late.

Eventually, we started thinking more seriously about distribution. We built a homeowner referral program, experimented with push notifications, created a realtor referral program, and tried to create more reasons for homeowners to come back.

But by then, we had spent a lot of time and money elsewhere.

The realtor program did not perform the way we hoped. The homeowner referral program didn't suddenly create a viral loop. Push notifications could re-engage users, but they couldn't make somebody's sink start leaking.

And eventually, we were out of money and running out of ideas.

## I thought product was the hard part

This was probably my biggest misconception as the technical founder.

I assumed building the product would be the hard part of building a technology company. I have a completely different level of respect for marketing and distribution now.

You can build something technically impressive. You can make it fast, beautiful, and easy to use. You can ship features every week.

None of that matters if you cannot repeatedly get the product into the hands of the right person at the right moment for an economically sustainable price.

For a while, I summarized our failure as:

> We never figured out organic growth.

I don't think that is quite right anymore.

Plenty of successful companies spend huge amounts of money on paid acquisition. Paid acquisition itself wasn't our problem. The real question was whether the economics worked.

Could the lifetime value of a homeowner or provider justify what it cost us to acquire the demand? Could we recover that cost quickly enough?

For Spade, we had a pretty brutal combination:

- Low-frequency consumer demand
- Local geographic constraints
- A two-sided marketplace
- App-download friction
- A need for immediate provider supply
- Limited monetization per transaction

And on top of all of that, we needed continuous growth.

We were not Facebook. People were not opening Spade because they were bored. Spade was transactional. You opened it when you needed something done.

That meant we constantly had to keep feeding new demand into the marketplace. A different consumer product might be able to survive with a stable group of highly engaged users.

We needed homeowners to continuously have new jobs.

The machine was hungry, and we never found a cheap enough way to feed it.

## We were also looking at the wrong metrics

One of the most obvious mistakes in hindsight was how often we celebrated metrics that were not the outcome we actually needed.

Impressions. Clicks. Website visitors. Downloads. Registered homeowners. Feature usage.

All useful information, but none of them were the signal.

The signal was:

**How many jobs are being posted?**

That was it.

If I were running Spade again, I would make almost every growth conversation work backwards from that number.

Not:

> How do we get more downloads?

But:

> How do we reduce the cost and time required to generate another qualified job post?

That question would have forced us to think differently much earlier.

## What I would do differently

The joke answer is:

**Don't build a marketplace.**

And there is some truth to that.

But I don't actually regret building one.

If I could go back, the biggest thing I would change is the order in which we solved problems. I would spend much less time asking whether people liked the idea and much more time understanding the frequency and severity of the problem.

I would validate distribution much earlier. Before spending months making the product better, I would want some answer to:

> Can we repeatedly create demand for this without relying on unlimited capital?

If I were building another low-frequency consumer product, I would also need a very clear answer for how the product remains relevant **between moments of need**.

That is the part we never solved with Spade.

Maybe that means content. Maybe it means home records or maintenance reminders. Maybe it means recurring services. Maybe it means partnerships or embedded distribution. Maybe it means the marketplace needs to be attached to a higher-frequency product entirely.

I don't know.

And I think it is important to admit that.

It is very easy to write a startup retrospective and pretend that, with the benefit of hindsight, you now know exactly what would have worked. I don't.

I know what I would test differently.

That is not the same thing.

## The lesson I'll carry forward

During Spade, we talked to investors and operators and watched an embarrassing number of startup videos. Everybody said some version of the same thing: marketplaces are hard, they are risky, they are capital intensive, and you have to solve multiple problems at the same time.

And three guys were sitting there thinking, _yeah, but we'll figure it out._

There is something kind of great about that.

Founders probably need a certain amount of ignorance, or maybe naïveté, to attempt things that rational people would tell them not to do. If I had understood every reason Spade might not work before we started, perhaps I wouldn't have started it.

Then I wouldn't have learned any of this.

So I don't regret the attempt. I am still incredibly proud of what we built.

But the biggest lesson Spade taught me had very little to do with code.

**Distribution is not something you figure out after you build the product. Distribution is part of the product.**

And for a marketplace, having users doesn't mean you have demand. You have demand when the right user needs something, in the right place, at the right time.

We built a marketplace that could work beautifully when that happened.

We just never figured out how to make it happen often enough.
